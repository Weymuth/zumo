📖 Section 1: The True Story of "Hello, World!"

The year was 1972. A brilliant computer scientist named Brian Kernighan was working at Bell Labs—the same place that invented the transistor and Unix. He was writing the very first official tutorial for a brand-new programming language called "B." The phrase was born in that little B tutorial — but it became world-famous six years later, when Kernighan reprinted it in the legendary 1978 book he wrote with Dennis Ritchie, The C Programming Language. C is the ancestor of the C++ you are about to write.

[IMAGE 1.18] The C Programming Language by Kernighan and Ritchie — the book that introduced "Hello, World!" (File: L01_IMAGE_1-18_kr_c_programming_book.png)

In that tutorial, he needed the simplest possible example—something that would prove the computer could run a program and talk back to the programmer. The version he typed in that 1972 B memo looked strange to modern eyes — B had no printf, so the message had to be spelled out a few characters at a time. Six years later, in the C book, it settled into the form the whole world has copied ever since:

main() {
    printf("hello, world\n");
}

That's it. Thirteen characters that said: "I work. We're ready to build the future."

Since that day, nearly every programming language has been introduced the same way — "Hello, World!" is the first program in the tutorials for Python, Java, JavaScript, C++, Rust, and Swift alike.

📘 Note
When you write your first program today, you're not just copying random code. You are exactly copying the very first programmers in history—the same people who invented the technology that runs YouTube, TikTok, Fortnite, and every phone in your pocket.

Welcome to the club. Now let's make your robot say hello!

📘 Note: How You Ask Is Part of the Answer

Your robot has a name, and in Section 9 you are going to go find out what it means. Some of those names are ordinary words — searching one on its own gets you nowhere useful.

Watch what a little context does. Search Marvin and you get a thousand people. Search "why would my robot be named Marvin?" and you land on the depressed robot from The Hitchhiker's Guide to the Galaxy. Same with Shakey — a strange adjective on its own, but ask it as a robot name and you meet the 1966 machine at Stanford Research Institute that was the first to reason about its own actions. Or Sojourner, which sounds like a word until you ask, and find NASA's first Mars rover, named for Sojourner Truth.

The words my robot are doing the work. You gave the search the context it was missing. That is a real skill, and you will use it every time you hit an error message in this course.

One warning. When an AI answers, it usually gets the fact right and then keeps going — guessing about your robot specifically. "Your robot is probably named this because it performs precise tasks…" It has never seen your robot. It is filling space.

Take the part that answers your question. Ignore the part that guesses about your situation. That habit matters more than any single command in this book — and it comes back in Lesson 3, when an autocomplete tool confidently offers you code for a function that does not exist.

📖 Section 3: Background Theory

3.0 What Makes a Robot a Robot?

Before we dive into the tools, let's answer a fundamental question: What actually makes something a robot?

Every robot—from your Zumo to a Mars rover to a factory assembly arm—follows the same basic pattern:

[GRAPHIC 1.11] The SENSE → DECIDE → ACT loop — every robot repeats this cycle forever (File: L01_GRAPHIC_1-11_sense_decide_act.svg)
  • SENSE: Gather information from the world (e.g., buttons, line sensors, proximity sensors, cameras) or internal states (e.g., battery voltage)
  • DECIDE: Your program chooses what to do based on that information
  • ACT: Make something happen (spin motors, play sounds, display messages)

This loop runs continuously—often 100 times per second or more. The robot senses, decides, acts... then senses again, decides again, acts again. Your code in loop() is what makes this happen.

So What ISN'T a Robot?

This is a great question to sharpen your understanding:

Thing Robot? Why?
Remote-control carNoA human makes all the decisions — the car just obeys
Your smartphoneNoIt computes, but doesn't physically act on the world autonomously
A toasterBarelyIt senses (heat) and acts (turns off), but can't really "decide"
A Roomba vacuumYes!It senses obstacles, decides where to go, and acts by moving
Your Zumo 32U4Definitely!It will sense lines, decide which way to turn, and act on its own
🔍 The Key Insight

The magic of robotics isn't in the hardware — it's in the DECIDE step. That's where your code lives. Your program is the robot's decision-making brain, and that's exactly what you'll learn to write in this course.

There is a difference between a machine that is automatic and one that is autonomous, and it is one of the most important ideas in this entire course.

An automatic machine follows a fixed script. A dishwasher sprays water for ten minutes, releases soap, rinses, and dries — all on a timer. It does not matter whether the dishes were already clean or caked in grime; the machine cannot tell the difference, because it never checks its own results. Engineers call this an open-loop system: instructions flow out, but no information ever flows back in.

An autonomous robot runs a closed-loop system. It acts, senses the result, and adjusts. When your Zumo fights a sumo match later in this course, it will not simply drive forward for five seconds and hope for the best — it will watch its line sensors, spot the ring edge, and steer away before it falls out. The Sense → Decide → Act cycle is closed by real-world feedback. That feedback loop is what separates a robot from an appliance.

3.1 What is PlatformIO?

Think of PlatformIO as a professional workshop for building robot code. It runs inside VS Code (Visual Studio Code), which is a powerful text editor used by millions of programmers worldwide.

🔑 PlatformIO — A professional development environment for programming microcontrollers. It handles compiling code, downloading libraries, and uploading to your robot.

Why not just use Arduino IDE? Great question! Arduino IDE is fine for simple projects, but PlatformIO gives you:

  • Better code completion — suggests function names as you type
  • Faster builds — compiles your code more efficiently
  • Professional tools — the same environment real engineers use
  • Better error messages — helps you find and fix problems faster
  • Library management — automatically downloads everything you need
💡 Why C++?

We program the Zumo in C++ because it's fast — critical for real-time robot control where even small delays could cause your robot to miss a sensor reading or crash into a wall. C++ lets you control exactly what the microcontroller does, with no wasted time.

📖 LEARN — From Text to Electricity: What the Compiler Does

The code you type is called source code. C++ uses English-like words such as void, while, and delay so that humans can read it. But the chip on your Zumo — the ATmega32U4 — cannot read text at all.

Deep down, a microcontroller is millions of microscopic electrical switches called transistors. Each switch knows exactly two states: voltage ON (a 1) or voltage OFF (a 0). The only language the chip speaks is long streams of those 1s and 0s, called machine code (or binary).

When you click the Build checkmark, you launch a program called the compiler — a strict, instant translator. It checks every character of your syntax, converts your logic into chip instructions, and packs the result into a compact binary file. Later, when you click Upload, that stream of 1s and 0s travels down the USB cable and into the robot's brain. You write in English-like text; the robot runs on electricity. The compiler is the bridge.

3.2 What's a Library?

🔑 Library — Pre-written code that provides ready-to-use functions for specific hardware or tasks.

Think of it like using a recipe instead of inventing cooking from scratch. The library is the recipe book—you still need to know how to follow the instructions, but you don't have to figure out everything yourself.

For the Zumo robot, we use the Zumo32U4 library from Pololu. It provides commands like:

  • motors.setSpeeds(200, 200) — move forward
  • buttonA.getSingleDebouncedPress() — wait for button press
  • display.print("Hello") — show text on OLED

Most of the time, a hardware library is written by the device's manufacturer — the people who designed the circuit board and know exactly how it is wired. That is the case here: Pololu built the Zumo, and Pololu wrote the Zumo32U4 library.

📖 LEARN — How the Library Connects Code to Board

The board is physical: the buzzer sits on one specific pin, each motor driver on others. The library knows that map. When you write motors.setSpeeds(200, 200), the library translates it into the exact electrical signals, on the exact pins, that this board's motor chips expect. Without the library you would need Pololu's wiring diagram and hundreds of lines of pin-level code just to spin a wheel. With it, one readable line does the job. That is the missing link between "board" and "library": the board is the hardware map, and the library is the driver who has memorized it.

3.3 What's a "Board"?

Your Zumo is built around an A-Star32U4 board, which contains an ATmega32U4 microcontroller—a tiny computer chip that controls all the robot's hardware.

🔑 A-Star32U4 — The brain of your Zumo robot. A small circuit board containing the ATmega32U4 microcontroller.
🔑 Microcontroller — A small, self-contained computer on a single chip that runs your program. Also called the "robot brain."
📘 Note

Throughout this book, we'll sometimes call the microcontroller the "robot brain" — they mean the same thing. It's the small computer that runs your program and controls everything else. When you see "brain," "microcontroller," or "controller board," we're talking about the same component.

5.0 Two Files, Two Jobs: platformio.ini First

Your project really has two files that matter: platformio.ini (the recipe card) and main.cpp (the actual program). Before reading the code, understand the recipe card — every line tells PlatformIO how to build for your robot:

[env:a-star32u4]
platform = atmelavr
board = a-star32u4
framework = arduino
monitor_speed = 115200
lib_deps = pololu/Zumo32U4@2.0.1
  • platform — the chip family (Atmel AVR — the family the ATmega32U4 belongs to)
  • board — the exact board, so the compiler knows its memory size and pin layout
  • framework — the coding style we use (Arduino: setup() and loop())
  • monitor_speed — how fast the Serial Monitor talks to the robot (must match the code)
  • lib_deps — the libraries to download — this is where the Pololu Zumo library comes from. The @2.0.1 on the end is a version pin: it names the exact release you want. Leave it off and the project quietly takes whatever the authors published most recently — and the robot you build in June stops matching the one you built in September. Pin it, and your build is the same build, forever.

5.1 The Include Statement

#include <Zumo32U4.h>

This line loads the Zumo library. It's like telling the compiler: "I'm going to use special Zumo commands, so please include all that code."

Where do special commands like motors, buzzer, and display come from? This line. #include <Zumo32U4.h> opens the Pololu library that lib_deps downloaded in your recipe card — it pours every Zumo command the manufacturer wrote into your program, ready to use.

5.2 Object Declarations

Zumo32U4ButtonA buttonA;
Zumo32U4OLED display;
Zumo32U4Motors motors;
Zumo32U4Buzzer buzzer;

These create objects — named connections to your robot's hardware:

  • buttonA — controls Button A on the robot
  • display — controls the small OLED screen
  • motors — controls both wheel motors
  • buzzer — makes sounds
🔑 Object — A named variable that represents a piece of hardware. Use the object's name to control that hardware.
📖 LEARN — What "Creating an Object" Really Means

This is your first taste of object-oriented programming. An object is a named bundle: some memory to track a piece of hardware, plus all the commands that work on it. Zumo32U4Motors motors; says: "build me a controller for the motor hardware, and let me call it motors." From then on, motors.setSpeeds(...) means "hey, motor controller — do your thing."

Why bother? Because your robot has many parts, and objects keep each part's commands attached to its name: buzzer. commands make sound, display. commands draw text, motors. commands move tracks. No mix-ups, no guessing. You will learn much more about objects in Lesson 7 — for now, read the pattern as name-dot-command: who, then what to do.

5.3 The setup() Function

void setup() {
    // Code here runs ONCE when robot powers on
}

The setup() function runs exactly once when your robot turns on or resets. Use it for initialization: setting up communication, displaying a welcome message, and running your test sequence.

📖 LEARN — Reading the Skeleton

Four small things carry a lot of meaning here:

  • void — means this function returns nothing. It just does its job; it does not hand a value back. (Functions that answer questions return values — later lessons.)
  • { } braces — the container. Everything between the opening { and closing } belongs to the function. Lose one and the compiler gets very confused.
  • // comments — anything after // on a line is a note for humans; the compiler ignores it completely. Good code talks to the next person who reads it.
  • the closing } — marks the exact end of the function. When the program reaches it, that function is done.

5.4 The loop() Function

void loop() {
    // Code here runs FOREVER, over and over
}

The loop() function runs forever after setup() finishes. For this lesson, we leave it empty because all our action happens in setup().

[GRAPHIC 1.12] How your program runs: setup() runs once, then loop() repeats forever (File: L01_GRAPHIC_1-12_setup_loop_flowchart.svg)
📘 Note: setup() vs loop()

Think of it this way:

  • setup() = "Do this once when I wake up" (like brushing your teeth in the morning)
  • loop() = "Keep doing this forever" (like breathing)

Most robot programs do their real work in loop(). In Lesson 1, we use setup() for our test sequence because we only want it to run once.

5.5 Complete Program

Here's the full program you'll upload:

// ───── THE TOOLBOX: library + hardware objects ─────
#include <Zumo32U4.h>
Zumo32U4ButtonA buttonA;
Zumo32U4OLED display;
Zumo32U4Motors motors;
Zumo32U4Buzzer buzzer;
// ───── RUNS ONCE AT POWER-ON ─────
void setup() {
    // Start Serial communication
    Serial.begin(115200);
    // Set up the display
    display.clear();
    display.setLayout21x8();
    display.print("Press A");
    // Wait for button press
    // ───── 1. WAIT FOR THE BUTTON ─────
    while (!buttonA.getSingleDebouncedPress()) {
        delay(10);
    }
    // Say hello to the Serial Monitor
    Serial.println("Hello, Robot!");
    // Beep!
    buzzer.playFrequency(440, 800, 15);
    delay(900);
    // Blink LED 3 times
    // ───── 2. BLINK THE LED ─────
    for (int i = 0; i < 3; i++) {
        ledYellow(1);
        delay(200);
        ledYellow(0);
        delay(200);
    }
    // Nudge forward and back
    display.clear();
    display.print("Moving!");
    // ───── 3. DRIVE: FORWARD, THEN BACK ─────
    motors.setSpeeds(200, 200);   // Forward
    delay(350);
    motors.setSpeeds(0, 0);       // Stop
    delay(200);
    motors.setSpeeds(-200, -200); // Backward
    delay(350);
    motors.setSpeeds(0, 0);       // Stop
    // Victory jingle!
    display.clear();
    display.print("Done!");
    buzzer.playFrequency(523, 100, 15);  // C5
    delay(150);
    buzzer.playFrequency(659, 100, 15);  // E5
    delay(150);
    buzzer.playFrequency(784, 300, 15);  // G5
}
// ───── RUNS FOREVER AFTER setup() ─────
void loop() {
    // Nothing here for Lesson 1
}

Three lines in this program do the heaviest lifting. You just read the whole thing — now slow down and reread these three the way an engineer would:

🔍 INSIGHT — The While-Loop Trapdoor

while (!buttonA.getSingleDebouncedPress()) looks like a magic waiting spell, but it is really a rapid-fire question. The library call reports true if the button was just pressed and false if it was not. The ! is the NOT operator — it flips that answer. While your finger is off the button, the check flips to true, so the loop runs again: check, wait 10 milliseconds, check again — about a hundred times every second. (The pace is set by the delay(10), not the chip: the ATmega32U4 ticks 16 million times per second and could check far faster — we deliberately slow it down.) The instant you press Button A, the answer flips to false, the trapdoor opens, and the program falls through to the next line. The robot was never frozen; it was asking "pressed yet?" over and over, faster than you can blink.

And one more word in that line earns its keep: Debounced. Inside the button is a tiny metal contact, and when you press it, that contact doesn’t close once — it physically bounces, chattering open and closed for a few milliseconds before it settles. Your finger never notices, but a chip checking sixteen million times a second absolutely does: without help, it would read one press as dozens of presses. "Debouncing" is the filter — here, built into the library call — that watches the chatter and reports one clean, single press. That’s the "Single" and the "Debounced" in getSingleDebouncedPress(): real engineering hiding in a function name.

🔍 INSIGHT — Anatomy of a For-Loop

for (int i = 0; i < 3; i++) squeezes three separate jobs into one line: initialize (int i = 0 — create a counter and start it at zero), check (i < 3 — before every pass, ask "is the counter still under 3?"), and increment (i++ — after every pass, add one to the counter).

Trace it exactly like the computer does: i is 0 — is 0 < 3? Yes → blink #1, counter becomes 1. Is 1 < 3? Yes → blink #2, counter becomes 2. Is 2 < 3? Yes → blink #3, counter becomes 3. Is 3 < 3? No → the loop ends and the program moves on to the motors. Change that one number and you change how many blinks you get — nothing else in the code has to move.

One more detail hiding in that loop: ledYellow(1) has no name-dot. The built-in LEDs are so simple and so foundational that the library skips the object and gives them direct commands — 1 means on, 0 means off. Everything else on the robot follows the name-dot-command pattern; the LEDs are the deliberate exception.

📖 LEARN — Sound Is Physics

buzzer.playFrequency(440, 800, 15) takes three values in order: frequency, duration, volume. The 440 means 440 Hertz — cycles per second. When this line runs, the chip drives the buzzer's pin through exactly 440 complete HIGH–LOW cycles every second. A small ceramic disc inside the buzzer flexes with every flip, shoving air molecules into pressure waves — and your ear reads those waves as the musical note A4, the same note orchestras use to tune. Change 440 to 880 and the chip flips twice as fast: the pitch jumps exactly one octave higher. Sound is not magic — it is electricity moving air on a schedule.

📘 Note: The Two-Second Sound Somebody Fought For

You are about to type a number and make a sound. It is worth knowing that somebody, somewhere, agonized over a sound just like this one.

In the early 1990s an Apple engineer named Jim Reekes could not stand the Macintosh startup chime. The problem was not that it sounded bad — it was that Macs crashed constantly, and the same family of tones played when they died. People had learned to associate the startup sound with failure. Turning your computer on made you flinch.

So he went home and recorded a new one on a synthesizer in his living room: a single C major chord, played with both hands stretched as wide as they would reach. Calm. Resolved. The opposite of a crash.

He did not have permission to change it. He got it into the machines with help from the engineers who controlled the ROM chips. When Apple found out and told him to take it back out, he refused, and offered a reason why removing it now would be too risky — a reason he later admitted he made up.

That chord shipped on Macs for years. Millions of people heard it every morning. One engineer decided two seconds of sound were worth the trouble, and was stubborn enough to win.

In Section 9 you will change 440 to a number you choose. It is a small thing. So was his.

📖 LEARN — How Fast Is Your Robot's Brain?

Processor speed is measured in clock ticks per second. Some context:

Intel Pentium (1993 desktop)66 MHz
Pentium 4 (2004 desktop)~3,000 MHz
Modern laptop / phone chip~3,000–4,000 MHz, many cores

Your robot's brain is humbler than a 1990s desktop — and that is exactly why we write lean C++ instead of heavyweight languages: every clock tick counts.

Step 6: Upload

📖 LEARN — Where Your Program Lives

Build translated your C++ into a binary file sitting on your computer. Upload — engineers call it "flashing" — copies that binary down the USB cable into the chip's flash memory, the same kind of storage a USB stick uses. Flash keeps its contents even with the power off, which is why your robot still remembers the program tomorrow, unplugged. The chip holds exactly one program at a time: every upload overwrites the last one, and whatever is in flash starts running automatically the moment you switch the robot on.

🔎 The Butterfly Error — and the Eight-Second Window

Sooner or later an upload will fail with a wall of red text like this:

Maybe it isn't a butterfly/AVR109 but a AVR910 device?
avrdude: initialization failed, rc=-1
avrdude: butterfly_recv(): programmer is not responding

Nothing is broken. Your robot has no separate programming chip. The same processor that runs your program has to reboot itself into a small helper program — the bootloader — to receive new code. The bootloader only waits about 8 seconds. If the upload tool does not reach it in time, the robot gives up and goes back to running your old program, and the tool is left talking to nobody. That is the entire error.

Fix 1 — the double-tap reset. Try this first. Press the robot's reset button twice, quickly — both taps within about three-quarters of a second. That tells the bootloader to stay awake and wait. Now click Upload. You have 8 seconds, which is plenty — but do not wander off.

Fix 2 — Clean, then upload again. Run PlatformIO's Clean, then upload. This works for a slightly sneaky reason: cleaning takes a few seconds, and those seconds are enough for the USB port to settle and reappear properly. You are not really fixing the build — you are buying time.

Start with Fix 1: it targets the actual cause and it is faster. Fix 2 is the dependable backup when the first one will not take.

Worth knowing: your robot shows up as one port while it is running your program, and a different port while the bootloader is awake. If uploads keep failing, unplug the USB cable, plug it back in, and give the port a moment to reappear before trying again.

💡 Common Pitfalls — Lesson 1
  1. Power-only USB cable: Upload fails silently. If it doesn't work, try a different cable — some only carry power, not data.
  2. Wrong board selected: PlatformIO should auto-detect, but if uploads fail, verify you're targeting the A-Star 32U4 / Zumo 32U4.
  3. Power switch off: The Zumo's tiny power switch is easy to miss. Robot uploads fine but doesn't move? Check the switch!

📖 LEARN — Five Notes That Said Hello

In 1977, Steven Spielberg had a problem. He needed humans and an alien ship to talk to each other with no shared language, nothing written, nothing spoken. He went to composer John Williams and asked for a musical phrase that could do the job.

Williams wanted seven notes. Spielberg insisted on five — any longer, he said, and it stopped sounding like a greeting. What he wanted was a doorbell.

So Williams asked a mathematician how many five-note sequences you could build from a twelve-note scale. The answer came back at roughly 134,000. He wrote about 350 of them. Then he and Spielberg sat in a room and listened to them one at a time until they found the one you know.

Here it is, the version played at Devil's Tower:

Note G A F F (low) C
Hz 392 440 349 175 262

Williams pointed out one thing about his own phrase that is easy to miss: it is left hanging on the fifth note. It does not resolve. It sounds like a question that expects an answer — which, in the film, is precisely what it is.

Two things worth taking from that. A search space of 134,000 got narrowed to one by listening — the same way you will find your Kp value in Lesson 8 and your PID gains in Lesson 15. And five notes were enough to carry a meaning, because somebody chose them on purpose.

Challenge 10 asks you to write five of your own. Not a tune — a message.

Does it drift? Which way? Hold on to that answer — Lesson 3 gives you TRIM, whose entire job is fixing it.

The left track pushes forward while the right pulls backward, so the robot rotates around its own center — a pirouette. This differential trick is exactly how the Zumo will steer for the rest of the course.

🔓 Click to reveal hint

The Zumo library has a function called readBatteryMillivolts() that returns the battery voltage in millivolts.

  • Fresh off the charger (NiMH): ~5,400 mV (5.4 V)
  • Healthy working range: ~4,800 mV — four cells sitting on their 1.2 V plateau
  • Low: below ~4,200 mV (1.05 V per cell) — recharge now; NiMH cells are damaged by draining past this
  • On alkalines the same robot reads ~6,300 mV fresh. Different chemistry, different numbers — see Lesson 3.

One catch: the reading is only honest when the robot is running on its batteries with the power switch ON. On USB power alone the number reads low and strange — that's the cable, not dead batteries.

Add this code at the beginning of setup(), after Serial.begin():

int voltage = readBatteryMillivolts();
Serial.print("Battery: ");
Serial.print(voltage);
Serial.println(" mV");
if (voltage < 4500) {
    display.clear();
    display.print("LOW BATT!");
    buzzer.playFrequency(200, 1000, 15);  // Low warning tone
    delay(2000);
}

This is a preview of how robots sense their environment—including themselves!

📖 Glossary
🔑 A-Star32U4 — The brain of your Zumo robot. A small circuit board containing the ATmega32U4 microcontroller.
🔑 Git — A version control tool that PlatformIO uses to download libraries.
🔑 Library — Pre-written code that provides ready-to-use functions for specific hardware or tasks.
🔑 Microcontroller — A small, self-contained computer on a single chip that runs your program. Also called the "robot brain."
🔑 Object — A named variable that represents and controls a piece of hardware.
🔑 PlatformIO — A professional development environment for programming microcontrollers.
🔑 Sense-Decide-Act Loop — The fundamental pattern all robots follow: gather information, make a decision, take action, and repeat.
🔑 Serial Monitor — A tool that displays text messages sent from your robot to your computer.

Note Frequencies

Two octaves of naturals, for buzzer.playFrequency(frequency, duration, volume). Frequency is in Hertz, duration in milliseconds, volume 0–15. Doubling the frequency raises the pitch exactly one octave — 440 and 880 are both A.

Note Hz

One note below this range is worth knowing: F3 = 175 Hz, the low F in the five-note phrase from Section 9.

Two ways to name a note. playFrequency() takes a number in Hertz, which is what these challenges use. playNote() takes a named constant instead, such as NOTE_A(4), if you would rather think in note names. play() takes a whole melody as a string.

⚠ play() runs the melody in the background — your program keeps running while it plays. That is useful, and it is also why notes fired back-to-back with no delay() collapse into a single blip.
