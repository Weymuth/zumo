📖 Section 1: The Robot That Learned to See

Lesson 3 ended with a promise. Your robot could finally drive straight — you measured its crooked tendencies, calculated a TRIM value, and corrected them — but it was still blind. It moved through the world without knowing a single thing about it. The last words of that lesson were: "Get ready to give your robot eyes!"

Here they are. Flip your robot over and look at the front edge of the underside, just behind the blade. You will see a row of five small rectangular windows. Each one is an eye — not a camera, but something simpler, faster, and perfectly suited to a robot that lives an inch above the floor. Each window contains an infrared reflectance sensor: a tiny invisible-light flashlight paired with a light detector. The flashlight shines infrared light straight down at the floor. The detector measures how much bounces back. A white surface reflects most of the light; a black surface swallows it. That single trick — measuring the bounce — is enough to tell your robot exactly where a black line is underneath it.

It is hard to overstate how much this one ability unlocks. Line following, which you will master in Lesson 8, is built entirely on these five sensors. Edge detection — knowing when the floor is about to end — comes from the same hardware. Nearly every task on a competition field, from staying inside a boundary to navigating an intersection, begins with the question "what is under me right now?" The humble reflectance sensor is the workhorse of competitive robotics, and after today it works for you.

But there is a twist, and it is the reason this lesson is shaped the way it is. Count the windows again: five. Now here is the surprising part — as your robot sits right now, only three of those five eyes are actually connected to its brain. Sensors 1, 3, and 5 (left edge, center, right edge) are wired up and ready. Sensors 2 and 4 are asleep — physically present, fully functional, and connected to nothing.

This is not a defect, and nobody forgot to finish building your robot. It is a deliberate engineering decision, and the reason is scarcity. The front sensor board actually carries eight sensors — the five line sensors facing down, plus three proximity sensors facing out (you will meet those in Lesson 5). But the board only has six wires available to carry sensor signals to the microcontroller. Eight sensors, six wires. Something has to give. Pololu's engineers chose a default — three line sensors down, three proximity sensors out — and then did something genuinely great: they put the choice on a pair of movable jumpers and handed it to you.

So this lesson is a story in two acts. In Act One, you will wake up the three connected sensors, learn to read them, and calibrate them so they give honest answers on your specific test surface. Then you will push them to their limit and measure — precisely — what three eyes can and cannot see. In Act Two, you will move two jumpers the size of a grain of rice, wake the other two eyes, and run the exact same tests again. By the end you will not just believe that five sensors beat three; you will have the numbers to prove it, and you will know exactly what those two extra eyes cost you in return. That is what real engineering looks like: not choosing what sounds better, but measuring the trade-off and deciding with evidence.

This is not busywork — Lesson 5 uses the side-facing proximity sensors, and those sensors share wires with line sensors 2 and 4. Leave the jumpers moved, and next week your robot is blind on its left and right sides. Engineers restore what they borrow. It's in the exit checklist, and yes, it will be checked.

📖 Section 3: Background Theory — How Robots See Lines

3.1 How Reflectance Sensors Work

Every one of the five windows on your robot's underside contains the same two components: an infrared LED that shines invisible light straight down, and a phototransistor — a light-sensitive switch — that watches for whatever bounces back. Infrared is just light with a wavelength slightly too long for human eyes. Your eyes can't see it, but a phototransistor can, and so can most phone cameras: point your phone at the underside while a sensor program runs and you may see the emitters glowing faint purple.

The physics of the measurement is beautifully simple. A white or light surface reflects most of the infrared light back up into the phototransistor. A black surface — like electrical tape — absorbs almost all of it, so very little returns. Measure the bounce, and you know the darkness of the floor under that window. Do it with five windows in a row, and you know where a dark line sits beneath the robot.

Now for the clever part: how the robot measures the bounce. There is no light-meter chip in these sensors. Instead, the microcontroller plays a timing game with each phototransistor, thousands of times per second:

  1. The pin charges the sensor's tiny capacitor — like filling a small bucket of electricity.
  2. The pin lets go and starts a stopwatch.
  3. The phototransistor drains the bucket — and it drains faster when more infrared light is hitting it.
  4. The stopwatch stops when the bucket is empty. That time, in microseconds, IS the sensor reading.

Over a bright white surface, lots of light returns, the bucket drains fast, and the reading is a small number — maybe 100–300 microseconds. Over black tape, almost no light returns, the bucket drains slowly, and the reading is a big number. If the bucket still isn't empty after 2000 microseconds, the robot gives up and calls it 2000 — the darkest reading possible.

Wait — the readings seem backward?

They do, and it trips up almost everyone once. Your instinct says a sensor should report "how much light" — big number, bright surface. These sensors report how long the drain took — so big numbers mean dark and small numbers mean bright. Say it to yourself now, because you will need it every time you read the numbers on the OLED: HIGH = DARK. LOW = LIGHT. On a white poster board with a black tape line, the sensor over the tape is the one shouting the biggest number.

🔑 Key Term: Reflectance Sensor

A sensor that measures how much light bounces back off a nearby surface. The Zumo's line sensors pair an infrared emitter with a phototransistor and report the reflection as a decay time in microseconds: high values mean a dark (absorbing) surface, low values mean a light (reflecting) surface.

📖 LEARN: Why infrared instead of regular light?

Three reasons. First, IR LEDs and phototransistors are cheap and tiny — the whole sensor fits in a package smaller than a grain of rice. Second, by shining its own light source, the sensor works in a dark room, a bright room, or inside the shadow under the robot — it isn't at the mercy of the ceiling lights. Third, pairing the emitter and detector at the same wavelength filters out most ordinary room light.

Most — not all. Sunlight is loaded with infrared. Direct sun on your test surface can flood the phototransistors and make every reading look "bright" no matter what's below. If your robot works perfectly in the classroom and goes haywire next to a sunny window, the window is the bug. (This is worth remembering: it shows up again in Section 8.)

3.2 One Array, Eight Sensors, Six Wires

Here is the part of the story most tutorials skip, and it explains the mystery from Section 1. The front sensor board on your Zumo carries eight sensors: the five line sensors facing down, plus three proximity sensor receivers facing outward — left, front, and right — which detect nearby objects (Lesson 5 is all about them). Eight sensors that would each love their own wire into the microcontroller.

They can't have it. A microcontroller has a fixed number of input/output pins, and on a robot as packed as the Zumo — motors, encoders, buzzer, display, buttons, USB — pins are a scarce resource, budgeted like money. The designers allocated six signal wires to the front sensor board. Six wires, eight sensors. Four sensors get a permanent wire of their own. The other four sensors share two wires, two apiece — and a physical jumper on the sensor board decides, for each shared wire, which sensor is actually plugged in.

Read that table slowly, because the whole lesson lives inside it. Line sensors 1, 3, and 5 are always wired — that is why they work out of the box. Line sensors 2 and 4 each share a wire with one of the side proximity receivers, and a jumper the size of a grain of rice sits on each shared wire like a railroad switch, routing the wire to one sensor or the other. The front proximity receiver keeps its own dedicated wire no matter what — the robot never gives up its forward vision.

Your robots arrive from the factory with both jumpers set to the proximity side: three line sensors down, three proximity receivers out. Move both jumpers to the line-sensor side and all five line sensors come alive. What exactly that costs you in proximity sensing is a question we will put to the test with real hardware rather than settle on paper — but one fact is already certain and non-negotiable: Lesson 5's programs are written for the factory jumper position. That is why this lesson ends by putting the jumpers back.

Both jumpers moved to the line-sensor (DN) side — the five-sensor position. You make this move in Section 6, not now.

🔍 INSIGHT: This is what engineering actually is

Beginners imagine engineering as building the best possible machine. Real engineering is allocating scarce resources — pins, grams, dollars, milliseconds, battery capacity — among competing goods. The Zumo's designers could not give you every sensor at once, so they gave you a choice and made it reversible. Learning to see designs as bundles of trade-offs, rather than lists of features, is one of the biggest mental upgrades in this entire course.

3.3 The Factory Default: Three Eyes Open

Why did Pololu pick three line sensors + three proximity receivers as the default, rather than five and one? Because it is the most balanced general-purpose loadout. The Zumo was born as a sumo robot — a machine that shoves opponents out of a white-bordered ring. For that job you need to know two things: where is the edge of the ring? (line sensors — and three are enough to catch a thick border) and where is my opponent? (proximity receivers, ideally on all three sides). The default configuration answers both questions at once.

In the three-sensor configuration, your working sensors are 1, 3, and 5 — far left, dead center, far right. Notice what that pattern gives you: coverage at the edges and the middle, the three places a boundary or line is most likely to matter first. And notice what it costs you: big blind gaps between sensor 1 and 3, and between 3 and 5, where a narrow line of tape can hide completely. You are going to find those gaps yourself, with your own robot, in Section 6 — and that discovery is what motivates Act Two.

3.4 Calibration: Teaching Your Sensors What "Black" Means

Suppose sensor 3 reads 1400 right now. Is that a black line? You genuinely cannot say. Maybe it's black tape. Maybe it's gray carpet with the robot sitting slightly nose-down so the sensor is closer to the floor. Maybe sensor 3 just runs "darker" than its siblings — and it might: no two phototransistors are manufactured perfectly identical, and a reading also shifts with surface texture, sensor height, even dust on the window. Raw readings are honest, but they are not comparable — not between sensors, and not between surfaces.

Calibration fixes this, and the idea is one you already use in daily life. Before judging whether today is "hot," you need to know the local range — 90°F is a heat wave in Alaska and a relief in Phoenix. Calibration shows each sensor its personal extremes: during a calibration sweep, you slide the sensors back and forth across the line while each one records the brightest value it ever sees (your white poster board) and the darkest (your black tape). From then on, every raw reading is re-graded on that personal scale and reported as a number from 0 to 1000: 0 means "as bright as the brightest thing I've ever seen," 1000 means "as dark as the darkest."

After calibration, the numbers finally mean the same thing everywhere. A calibrated 950 is a confident "black" whether it came from sensor 1 or sensor 5, on poster board or on a competition mat. And that is what makes everything in the rest of this lesson possible — including the one-number line position you are about to meet.

🔑 Key Term: Calibration

Measuring a sensor's actual minimum and maximum on the current surface, then rescaling all future readings onto a standard 0–1000 range. Calibration data lives in RAM — it evaporates at power-off, which is why every run of your program starts with a fresh calibration sweep. New surface or new lighting? Recalibrate.

💡 Tip

When teams complain that "the sensors are broken," the real story — nine times out of ten — is a bad or skipped calibration sweep. A sweep that never actually passed every sensor over the tape leaves some sensor thinking dark-gray is the blackest thing in the universe. Sweep slowly, and make sure every window crosses the line.

3.5 From Five Numbers to One: How readLine() Thinks

Once the sensors are calibrated, your program could look at all the individual values — sensor 1 says 40, sensor 2 says 130, sensor 3 says 880... — and reason about them one by one. Sometimes you will. But for steering, what you really want is a single answer to a single question: "where is the line under me, exactly?" The library function readLine() compresses all five readings into that one number, and the way it does it is a genuinely elegant piece of math called a weighted average.

First, each sensor gets a position label: sensor 1 sits at position 0, sensor 2 at 1000, sensor 3 at 2000, sensor 4 at 3000, sensor 5 at 4000. Then readLine() computes the average of those positions — but each position is weighted by how dark its sensor reads. A sensor screaming "1000 — pure black!" pulls the average hard toward its own position. A sensor whispering "60 — basically white" barely tugs at all. The result is the line's center of darkness, on a scale from 0 (far left) to 4000 (far right), with 2000 meaning dead center.

Work one example by hand — this is exactly the arithmetic the robot does hundreds of times per second. Say the calibrated readings are:

Position = (120,000 + 1,700,000 + 1,920,000) ÷ (120 + 850 + 640) = 3,740,000 ÷ 1,610 ≈ 2323. The line's darkness is centered a little right of sensor 3 — leaning toward sensor 4, which matches the readings: sensor 4 is fairly dark, sensor 2 barely. Notice something remarkable: the answer 2323 is a position between sensors. The weighted average lets five sensors report line positions with far finer grain than five separate yes/no votes ever could.

Three fine-print behaviors of readLine() are worth knowing now, because each one will explain something you'll see on the bench:

  • It ignores whispers. Values of 50 or below are treated as noise and left out of the average entirely, so a speck of dust doesn't drag the position around.
  • It remembers which side it fell off. If no sensor sees anything dark (nothing above 200), the line is lost — and instead of panicking, readLine() returns 0 if the line was last seen left of center, or 4000 if it was last seen to the right. The robot's best guess is "it went that way." Lesson 8 turns this little memory into recovery behavior.
  • The scale depends on the sensor count. Positions run 0 to (number of sensors − 1) × 1000. Five sensors: 0–4000, center 2000. Three sensors: 0–2000, center 1000. Keep this in mind in Act One.

One more relative: readLineWhite() does the identical math with every value flipped, for the mirror-world case of a white line on a dark floor. Same idea, opposite wardrobe.

3.6 Resolution: What Two More Eyes Actually Buy

So why bother with five sensors if three work? The answer is a concept that shows up everywhere in engineering — screens, cameras, GPS, medical scanners — called resolution: the level of detail a measurement can distinguish.

Think about the blind gaps first. With three active sensors at the far left, center, and far right of the array, there are two wide stretches of floor between neighboring sensors that nobody is watching. A strip of electrical tape is narrow; it can sit entirely inside one of those gaps, and the robot reads all white — the line is directly underneath it and completely invisible. Wake sensors 2 and 4 and those two big gaps become four small ones; the same tape now cannot hide, because it always overlaps at least one window.

Then there's the quality of the position estimate itself. The weighted average is only as good as its ingredients: with three sensors contributing, the estimate leans on sparse information and jumps in coarser steps as the line moves; with five, each small shift of the line changes the balance among more witnesses, and the position number tracks it more smoothly and more confidently. In Lesson 8, when that position number gets fed into a steering correction many times per second, the difference between a jumpy estimate and a smooth one becomes the difference between a robot that slaloms down the line and one that glides. And in Lesson 15, an algorithm called PID will squeeze every drop of value out of that smoothness.

But — and this is the honest, textbook-grade truth — resolution is not free, and more is not automatically better. You already know the price tag on the Zumo (two jumpers' worth of proximity hardware). There are subtler costs too: five sensors take longer to read than three, and more data per loop means more work per loop. For a thick line and simple decisions, three sensors may be genuinely sufficient — plenty of successful sumo robots never wake sensors 2 and 4 at all. The point of Act Two is not "five is better." The point is that after today, you will know how much better, for what, and at what cost — because you measured it.

🔑 Key Term: Resolution

The smallest difference a measurement system can distinguish. More sensors across the same space = finer spatial resolution: smaller things can be detected, and positions can be pinned down more precisely. Resolution always costs something — pins, time, money, or power — so engineers buy exactly as much of it as the job needs.

4.2 Find the Two Jumpers

Stay in the same pose and look at the front sensor board — the separate small circuit board that carries the sensor windows. ⚠️ WARNING — Look, don't touch (yet)

4.3 Build Your Test Surface

Every measurement in this lesson happens on a surface you build in the next five minutes, and the quality of your data depends on it. You need three things: strong contrast, a flat surface, and no direct sunlight.

  3. Leave generous white margins — at least a robot's width of clean white on each side of the line, so sensors far from the tape have honest "bright" ground to look at.

5.1 The Shape of Today's Program

Both acts of this lesson run the same program in two costumes. In Act One it wakes three sensors and shows their calibrated readings. In Act Two you change two lines and a display function, and it wakes five sensors and adds a line-position readout. Nothing in it drives the motors — today's program only senses and shows. The program has five jobs, and each one maps to a helper or a section of main.cpp: declare the hardware, wake the right sensor set, calibrate, read into an array, and display. Let's take them in order.

5.4 Waking the Sensors: The Software Half of the Jumper Story

The jumpers are the hardware half of the three-vs-five choice. The software half is a single line in setup():

Notice the asymmetry, because it matters twice today. initThreeSensors() is safe everywhere — its three sensors are hardwired. initFiveSensors() makes a promise about the hardware — that pins 20 and 4 actually lead to line sensors. The code compiles either way; the compiler cannot see your jumpers. If the promise is false, nothing crashes and nothing warns you — sensors 2 and 4 simply report garbage. You will see this with your own eyes in Section 6, on purpose, because it is one of the most important lessons in robotics: hardware and software each keep promises the other can't check.

5.5 Reading Into an Array — Three Values, One Variable

Lesson 3 promised that the line sensors would hand you data "as an array." Here is that moment. One reading produces three numbers at once (five in Act Two) — one per sensor. Rather than juggling three separate variables, the program declares a single array:

// ===== GLOBAL VARIABLES =====
unsigned int lineSensorValues[NUM_SENSORS];   // one slot per active sensor

An array is a row of numbered boxes stored under one name. lineSensorValues[NUM_SENSORS] creates NUM_SENSORS boxes; a call like lineSensors.read(lineSensorValues) fills all of them in one shot. You then open an individual box with its index in square brackets — and here is the rule that catches everyone at least once: the boxes are numbered starting from ZERO. The first box is lineSensorValues[0], not [1].

And that creates a genuinely tricky mismatch, because the sensors are numbered 1–5. Worse: in the three-sensor configuration, the array only has boxes for the sensors that are awake. Study this table until it feels obvious — it is the number-one source of Lesson 4 bugs:

⚠️ WARNING — Index is not sensor number

In the three-sensor configuration, the center sensor lives in box [1]. In the five-sensor configuration, the center moves to box [2]. Code that reads "the center sensor" by index must change when the configuration changes. Say it out loud: index counts awake sensors from zero; it does not name the sensor.

🔑 Key Term: Array

A numbered row of same-type values stored under one name. Declared with a size (unsigned int values[3];), accessed by index in square brackets, and — always — indexed from zero. Arrays let one function call hand you many results at once, and let one loop process them all.

5.7 The Calibration Sweep

/*
calibrateSensors()
------------------
Shows each sensor its personal brightest and darkest.
Slide the robot side to side across the line until the
countdown ends. Every window must cross the tape.
*/
void calibrateSensors() {
  display.clear();
  display.print(F("Sweep!"));
  unsigned long startTime = millis();
  while (millis() - startTime < CAL_TIME_MS) {
    lineSensors.calibrate();
    display.gotoXY(0, 1);
    display.print((CAL_TIME_MS - (millis() - startTime)) / 1000 + 1);
    display.print(F(" sec "));
  }
  display.clear();
  display.print(F("Cal done"));
  delay(750);
  display.clear();
}

This helper is Section 3.4 turned into code. For five seconds (that's CAL_TIME_MS), it calls lineSensors.calibrate() as fast as it can, while you slide the robot side to side across the tape. Each call takes a fresh reading on every sensor and quietly updates that sensor's personal record book — brightest ever seen, darkest ever seen. The while (millis() - startTime < CAL_TIME_MS) pattern is the non-blocking clock trick from Lesson 3's boot-banner fix, reused: check the elapsed time each pass instead of freezing in one long delay, which is what lets the countdown on row 1 stay live.

The record books are the whole product of calibration. They live in RAM, they vanish at power-off, and every future readCalibrated() uses them to re-grade raw readings onto the honest 0–1000 scale.

🔑 Section 8A: Deciding and Repeating — If Statements and For Loops

Everything so far has been sensing and showing. But the entire point of giving a robot eyes is so it can act on what it sees — and acting on information requires something new: a way for code to take different paths depending on a value. That tool is the if statement, and it is arguably the single most important concept in all of programming. Every behavior your robot will ever have — stop at the line, chase the light, avoid the wall, win the match — is if statements all the way down. The challenges in Section 9 use it immediately; this section makes sure you own it completely.

🔁 Builds on: the if statement you first read in Lesson 2 and first wrote yourself in Lesson 3, Section 5.5 . This is the full treatment — the same tool, now wired to sensors that change their mind forty times a second.

Then it does the same for the for loop — the tool you have already been using since Step 4 without anyone taking it apart for you. An if statement lets code choose. A for loop lets code repeat. Section 9 asks for both, and two of its challenges ask for them together.

(Your robot is back in the standard three-sensor configuration after Section 7.3 — every example here assumes that: center sensor = lineSensorValues[1], position scale 0–2000.)

8A.1 What Is a Decision in Code?

You already know the shape. An if statement puts a fork in an otherwise straight road: "IF this condition is true, run these lines. Otherwise, skip them." You make hundreds of these forks a day without noticing — if it's raining, take the umbrella; if the light is red, stop. Lesson 3 had you writing them against a number you typed yourself; from here on the number arrives from a sensor and changes while the robot is moving, which is where this gets interesting. Start with the anatomy, because every piece of it matters once the values stop holding still:

Three parts, always the same three parts. The condition, in parentheses — a question with a yes/no answer, usually built from a comparison: > greater than, < less than, == equal to (two equals signs — one is assignment, and that typo is a classic). The braces, which fence off the conditional block. The action — everything inside the fence, which runs only on "yes."

Reading it out loud helps more than you'd expect. if (sensorValue > 500) { ledYellow(1); } reads: "If the sensor value is greater than 500, turn the yellow LED on." If the value is 500 or below, the whole block is skipped as if it weren't there, and the program carries on underneath.

8A.2 Adding an Else

Often "skip it" isn't enough — you want a plan for both answers. else attaches the "no" plan:

if (sensorValue > 500) {
  ledYellow(1);   // dark detected - LED ON
} else {
  ledYellow(0);   // light detected - LED OFF
}

Exactly one of the two blocks runs, every time — never both, never neither. This on/off symmetry is everywhere in robotics: moving or stopped, line or no line, friend or wall.

🔑 Key Term: Threshold

The cutoff value a condition compares against — the 500 in sensorValue > 500. On the calibrated 0–1000 scale, 500 is the natural "darker than halfway" line between white and tape. Choosing good thresholds from real data — like the readings in your Step 4 table — is a skill you'll use in every sensor lesson from now on.

🔁 8A.6 Doing It Three Times Without Writing It Three Times: the for Loop

You have already written one of these. Back in Step 4 you typed this into loop(), and it worked, and nobody explained it:

for (uint8_t i = 0; i < NUM_SENSORS; i++) {
  Serial.print(lineSensorValues[i]);
  Serial.print('\t');
}

Here is what it saved you, and it is an argument this lesson can make better than any other in the book. Without a loop, printing three sensors means writing the same two lines three times, once for box [0], once for [1], once for [2]. Tedious, but survivable.

Then Act Two woke sensors 2 and 4. Five sensors. Every one of those copied blocks would have to be found and duplicated again — and every place in your program that walked the array would need the same surgery, and the one you missed would fail silently. Instead you changed a single character: NUM_SENSORS = 3 became 5. The loop asked for the new number and kept working. That is what the loop bought you, and you already spent it today without noticing.

The three parts live inside the parentheses, separated by semicolons, and they do not run in reading order. Trace one lap at a time:

Forget the i++ and the counter never moves: the test stays true forever and your robot freezes with the screen half-drawn. Put a semicolon right after the closing parenthesis — for (...); — and the loop runs an empty body the correct number of times and then executes your block once. Both compile clean. Neither is a compiler's problem to catch; they are yours.

🔁 8A.7 A Loop with a Decision Inside

An if statement chooses. A for loop repeats. Put one inside the other and you get the shape most real robot code is made of — and you already own an example of it. This is showReadings() from Section 5.8, the function drawing your OLED right now:

for (uint8_t i = 0; i < NUM_SENSORS; i++) {
  unsigned int scaled = lineSensorValues[i] / 10;
  if (scaled > 99) scaled = 99;
  if (scaled < 10) display.print('0');
  display.print(scaled);
  if (i < NUM_SENSORS - 1) display.print(' ');
}

Three ifs, all inside one loop, each answering a question about this lap only. The last one is the interesting one: i < NUM_SENSORS - 1 is true on every lap except the final one, so a space goes between the numbers and never after the last. That single line is why your display reads 42 87 13 and not 42 87 13 with a stray gap pushing the row one character too wide.

Notice what the loop and the if are each responsible for. The loop decides how many times. The if decides what happens this time. Keep those two jobs straight and nested code stops being confusing — and you will need them together within the hour, because two of the Section 9 challenges are exactly this shape.

🔁 8A.8 Remembering Where You Were: a bool as Memory

Every program you have written so far answers the same question over and over: what is true right now? The sensor is dark, so the LED is on. The sensor is light, so the LED is off. Ask again a tenth of a second later and the answer is fresh. Nothing is remembered, and nothing needs to be.

Now try to count something. Slide a strip of tape under the center sensor and count how many times it passes. The obvious code is a disaster:

// WRONG - this counts hundreds per crossing
if (lineSensorValues[1] > 600) {
    crossings++;
}

The loop runs about ten times a second. The tape sits under the sensor for perhaps half a second. That is five trips through the if, all of them true, all of them adding one — for a single crossing. The count runs away and the number on the display is meaningless.

The bug is not in the comparison. The comparison is right: the sensor really is over the tape, five times in a row. The bug is that you asked the wrong question. You do not want to know whether the sensor is on the line. You want to know whether it just arrived — and "just arrived" is not a fact about this instant. It is a fact about this instant compared to the last one.

</> A bool that outlives one trip through the loop
bool — true or false, a yes/no switch — you met it in Lesson 2 §3.2b as a type. Here it takes a job: holding one fact from one loop into the next.

A variable declared above loop() — in GLOBAL VARIABLES — is created once and keeps its value forever. A variable declared inside loop() is born and dies on every trip. That placement is the whole difference between a robot that remembers and one that cannot.

So keep a flag that records where you were, and compare it to where you are:

// ===== GLOBAL VARIABLES =====
bool onLine = false;      // where I was last time
int  crossings = 0;

// inside loop():
bool nowDark = (lineSensorValues[1] > 600);   // where I am now

if (nowDark && !onLine) {   // dark now, light before -> just ARRIVED
    crossings++;
}
onLine = nowDark;           // today becomes yesterday

Read the middle line out loud: "if it is dark now and it was not dark before." That is an edge — the moment a value changes, not the stretch of time it stays changed. The five true readings are still five true readings, but only the first of them has a false onLine sitting behind it, so only the first one counts.

The last line is the one people forget, and forgetting it breaks everything in a way that is hard to see: without onLine = nowDark; the flag never updates, the second half of the test is true forever, and you are back to counting hundreds. The flag has to be brought up to date at the end of every trip, whether anything happened or not.

📘 Note

Real sensors do not switch cleanly. Right at the threshold a reading can flicker across the line several times in a few milliseconds, and each flicker is a fresh "arrival." The cure is to arm and disarm at two different values — count when it climbs past 600, but do not re-arm until it drops below 400. The gap between them is a no-man's-land that noise cannot rattle around in. That is hysteresis, and you will meet it again wherever a robot has to decide that something has genuinely changed.

🔁 8A.9 "Close Enough": abs() and the Deadband

Some questions are not "is it dark?" but "how far off am I?" Ask the line sensors for a position with readLine() and you get a number from 0 to 2000, with 1000 meaning dead center. Subtract, and you have the most useful quantity in robotics:

int error = position - 1000;   // how far off centre, and which way

That single subtraction carries two facts at once. Its size says how wrong you are; its sign says which way. Position 1400 gives error = 400; position 600 gives error = -400. Equally wrong, opposite directions — which is exactly what you need to know to correct it.

But now you want to ask "am I close enough to stop?" and the sign gets in the way. error < 100 is true for −400, which is not close at all. You need the size without the sign:

</> abs() — throw away the sign, keep the size
abs(x) — the absolute value of x: how far it is from zero, always positive — abs(-400) is 400, and so is abs(400).

Use it whenever you care how far off something is but not which side it is off on. Keep the signed value too — you still need the sign to know which way to correct.

So abs(error) < 100 asks the real question: am I within 100 of centre, on either side?

Why not simply demand error == 0? Because the robot will never get there, and it will destroy itself trying. Position 1002 is off by 2, so it corrects. The correction overshoots to 997, so it corrects the other way. Overshoot, correct, overshoot, correct — the robot buzzes in place around the target it cannot quite land on, forever. It is not broken. It is doing precisely what you asked, and what you asked was impossible.

</> The deadband — a band where the robot does nothing
A deadband is a tolerance around the target inside which you take no action — if (abs(error) < DEADBAND) { motors.setSpeeds(0, 0); }

Too narrow and the robot hunts and buzzes. Too wide and it stops visibly off-target and calls it done. The right width is a number you find by trying, not one you can look up — it depends on your sensors, your surface, and your speed.

Put the three ideas in order and you have a control loop: measure the error, ignore it if it is small enough, otherwise push in the direction that shrinks it. Every steering correction in this book is that shape.

Right now the correction is blunt: off-centre, so pivot. In Lesson 8 you will make the correction proportional to the error — a small error gets a gentle nudge, a big one a hard shove — and the robot stops lurching and starts gliding. The error = position - 1000 line you write in Challenge 5 today is the same line that becomes line following four lessons from now. This is not a warm-up for it; it is it, without a name yet.

  motors.setSpeeds(-TURN_SPEED, TURN_SPEED);   // line is left -> pivot left
} else if (error > DEADBAND) {

} else {
  motors.setSpeeds(0, 0);                      // centered - hold
}

The phototransistor can't tell WHOSE infrared it's drinking. Sunlight is full of IR, so the bucket drains fast no matter what — everything reads "bright." Your emitters were never the only light source in the room; they're just usually the loudest.

📖 Glossary
Infrared (IR) — Light with a wavelength just beyond red — invisible to human eyes, plainly visible to the robot's sensors (and to most phone cameras). The line sensors both emit and detect it.
Reflectance sensor — An emitter/detector pair that shines IR at a surface and measures how much bounces back. Bright surfaces reflect a lot; dark surfaces absorb. All five floor-facing windows are reflectance sensors.
Phototransistor — The detector half of each sensor — a light-controlled valve. The more IR that lands on it, the faster it drains the sensor's measurement charge.
Raw reading — The uncalibrated drain time, 0–2000 microseconds. HIGH = DARK. Only meaningful relative to that one sensor's personal range — remember the 1400 problem.
Calibration — The sweep that teaches each sensor its personal brightest and darkest on the actual surface. Produces per-sensor record books, stored in RAM, gone at power-off.
Calibrated reading — A raw reading re-graded against the record books onto a shared 0 (brightest seen) to 1000 (darkest seen) scale — the scale on which sensors finally agree.
Array — A numbered row of same-type values under one name — unsigned int values[3]. Filled all at once by a sensor read; accessed one box at a time by index.
Index — A box number in an array, counted from ZERO. Index is not sensor number: with three sensors awake, the center sensor lives at index [1].
Weighted average — How readLine() turns five (or three) readings into one position: each sensor's location, weighted by how much darkness it sees. It's why positions between sensors exist.
Resolution — How finely a measurement can distinguish positions. More sensors = smaller blind gaps and a smoother position estimate — you measured this in Section 7.
Jumper — A small shorting block that connects two of three pins, physically routing a shared microcontroller pin to one sensor or another. The Zumo has two, factory-set to the proximity side.
Threshold — The cutoff a condition compares against — sensorValue > 500. Choose it from real data, halfway between your measured white and dark readings.
Hysteresis — Using two thresholds (trigger high, release low) so a jittery reading can't rapidly flip a decision back and forth. The gap between them is a no-change zone.
If statement — The fork in the road: a condition in parentheses, braces around an action, and the action runs only when the condition is true. With else, both answers get a plan.
